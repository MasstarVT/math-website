# Unfolded

**Live site: https://masstarvt.github.io/math-website/**

Interactive visualizations of mathematics you can watch move, sorted into tabs. The front page is an index: pick a tab to read through one topic, or a card to jump straight to one. Search by name or idea ("sunflower", "π", "weather"), or filter by tags like recursion, randomness or surprises.

**Geometry**
- **The tesseract**: a 4D hypercube (plus the 16-cell and 24-cell) rotating through the fourth dimension, drawn as its 3D shadow.
- **The golden angle**: how sunflowers pack their seeds.
- **Penrose tiling**: fat and thin rhombuses that never repeat, with a fat-to-thin ratio closing in on the golden ratio.
- **Voronoi diagrams**: nearest-point cells you can drag, with the Delaunay triangulation.
- **Squeezing π**: polygons with 6, 12, 24, 48, 96 and more sides trapping π from inside and outside, the way Archimedes did.
- **The Euler line**: a triangle you can drag whose centroid, circumcenter and orthocenter always line up, with the nine-point circle.
- **Morley's trisector theorem**: cut the angles of any triangle into thirds and an equilateral triangle appears.
- **Pick's theorem**: build a polygon on a pegboard and find its area by counting pegs.
- **Conic sections**: a double cone cut by a tilting plane into circles, ellipses, parabolas and hyperbolas, with Dandelin spheres.
- **Circle inversion**: a line, a circle, a grid or your own drawing reflected in a circular mirror.
- **Rollers that aren't round**: Reuleaux triangles and other shapes of constant width carrying a level board, and drilling an almost square hole.
- **The Platonic solids**: the five regular solids morphing into their duals, and why there can't be a sixth.
- **Hyperbolic tilings**: {7,3}, {5,4} and other regular tilings of the Poincaré disk that you can slide around.
- **Wallpaper groups**: draw something and watch any of the 17 wallpaper groups repeat it across the plane.

**Graphs & topology**
- **The bridges of Königsberg**: try to cross every bridge exactly once, and see why the places with an odd number of bridges decide it.
- **The four color theorem**: paint a random map so no neighbors match, or watch a backtracking solver do it with four colors while three often fail.
- **The party problem**: color the lines between six guests in a game where a one-color triangle loses and a draw is impossible.
- **Untangle**: drag dots until no lines cross, level by level, then meet K₅ and K₃,₃, which can never be untangled.
- **Minimum spanning trees**: the shortest road network that joins draggable cities, built step by step by Kruskal's and Prim's methods.
- **The Möbius strip**: an ant walks both "sides" of a one-sided loop, and cutting it makes one long loop or two linked ones.
- **Euler's polyhedron formula**: V − E + F counted on solids, a torus and a double torus, and unchanged when every face is split.
- **The glued square**: a ship flies through the glued edges of a torus, a Klein bottle and a projective plane, and the torus folds into a doughnut.
- **The hairy ball theorem**: comb a hairy ball and its cowlicks never all go away, while a doughnut combs flat.
- **Opposite points**: a changing temperature always matches at two opposite points, around a ring and on a globe.

**Fractals**
- **The dragon curve**: copy the curve, rotate the copy 90° around its end, attach, repeat.
- **The Mandelbrot set**: zoomable, rendered on the GPU with WebGL.
- **The chaos game**: random jumps that build the Sierpinski triangle, other fractals and a fern.
- **The Koch snowflake**: infinite perimeter around a finite area, with a magnifier.
- **The Hilbert curve**: a line that fills a square, plus the closed Moore curve.
- **The Pythagoras tree**: squares and right triangles, where every generation adds up to the trunk's area.
- **Newton's fractal**: which root Newton's method finds from each starting guess, with draggable roots (WebGL).
- **The Apollonian gasket**: touching circles whose curvatures are all whole numbers.
- **Julia sets**: pick c by dragging on a small Mandelbrot map and watch its Julia set change, from Douady's rabbit to dust (WebGL).
- **The Menger sponge**: a cube with holes cut in it forever, turning in 3D, its volume shrinking to zero while its surface grows without limit.
- **Plants from rules**: L-systems, where a rewriting rule and a turtle with a pen grow plants and bushes, and you can mutate the rule.
- **The Cantor set**: middle thirds removed forever, with the devil's staircase that climbs from 0 to 1 while being flat almost everywhere.
- **The coastline paradox**: a fractal island measured with shorter and shorter rulers, its dimension read off a log-log plot.
- **The Weierstrass function**: a continuous curve with no slope anywhere, zoomed ten billion times beside a smooth one that flattens out.
- **Fractal mountains**: diamond-square landscapes in 3D, built round by round, with a roughness slider.
- **The Fibonacci word**: the never-repeating word 0100101001001… walked as a path that folds into squares within squares.
- **The Lyapunov fractal**: the logistic map with r taking turns between a and b, colored by order and chaos (WebGL).
- **Hofstadter's butterfly**: the allowed energies of an electron in a magnetic field, with the gaps colored by Chern number.

**Chaos**
- **Langton's ant**: two rules that build chaos, then a highway.
- **The butterfly effect**: the Lorenz attractor, with paths that start almost together and drift apart.
- **The Game of Life**: Conway's cellular automaton, with a glider gun, other starting patterns and a grid you can draw on.
- **The road to chaos**: the logistic map's bifurcation diagram, period doubling and the window of period 3.
- **The sandpile**: grains that topple at 4, poured at the center, and the pile that acts like zero.
- **Growing crystals**: diffusion-limited aggregation from a seed or from the ground.
- **The double pendulum**: a pendulum hung from a pendulum, with copies started a ten-thousandth of a degree apart that soon fly their separate ways.
- **The three-body problem**: three suns pulling on each other, from the stable figure eight to the Pythagorean problem, where one body is flung out for good.
- **Strange attractors**: Clifford and de Jong maps hopping a point twenty million times to build a glowing density picture.
- **Rule 30**: all 256 one-row cellular automata with their 8-cell rule key, from the randomness of Rule 30 to Rule 110 and Rule 184's traffic jams.
- **Flocking**: boids that follow three local rules and form flocks with no leader, scattering from your pointer like a hawk.
- **Predators and prey**: rabbits and foxes booming and crashing in turn, tracing closed loops in the Lotka–Volterra model.
- **Turing patterns**: two reacting, spreading chemicals that paint spots, stripes, dividing cells and coral, and you can paint too.
- **Fireflies in sync**: the Kuramoto model, where fireflies with their own rhythms fall into step once the coupling passes a critical strength.
- **Chaotic billiards**: balls on round, oval and stadium tables, where the stadium makes almost identical shots drift apart and fill the table.
- **Arnold's cat map**: a picture stretched and wrapped until it's static, which comes back whole after a number of steps that depends on the grid size.

**Chance**
- **The bell curve**: a Galton board filling bins that approach the normal distribution.
- **The law of large numbers**: coins flipped up to a million times, closing in on 50% heads while heads minus tails drifts apart.
- **The Monty Hall problem**: staying versus switching, with 3, 10 or 100 doors.
- **Buffon's needle**: estimating π by dropping needles on a lined floor, or darts at a circle in a square.
- **The birthday paradox**: random rooms of people against the exact chance of a shared birthday.
- **Benford's law**: the leading digits of powers of 2, Fibonacci numbers, factorials and random data.
- **Random walks**: walkers on a line and a flat grid always come home, but on a 3D lattice only about 34% ever do.
- **Gambler's ruin**: bet 1 chip at a time until you're broke or reach your goal, and see how a small house edge makes ruin nearly certain.
- **Percolation**: water poured through a grid of randomly open squares, and the sudden switch near 59.3% open where it starts getting through.
- **Bertrand's paradox**: three fair ways to draw a random chord give three different chances, 1/3, 1/2 and 1/4.
- **The sticker album**: filling an album of n random stickers takes about n × (1 + 1/2 + … + 1/n) packs, and the last few take the longest.
- **The 37% rule**: skip the first 37% of candidates, then take the best so far, and you pick the very best about 37% of the time.
- **Bayes' theorem**: why most positive results for a rare illness are false alarms, counted out on 1,000 people.
- **Markov chains**: a toy weather chain whose long-run mix of sunny, cloudy and rainy days is the same from any start.
- **Penney's game**: whatever run of three coin flips you pick, there's another that tends to turn up first.
- **Pólya's urn**: each draw adds a ball of the color drawn, and each urn settles on its own random mix.

**Numbers**
- **Times tables on a circle**: cardioids, nephroids and friends.
- **Prime spirals**: primes plotted at (n, n) in polar coordinates, making spirals and rays.
- **The Collatz conjecture**: thousands of 3n + 1 paths drawn backwards from 1 so they grow into a plant.
- **Recamán's sequence**: back if you can, forward if you must, drawn as arcs, with optional sound.
- **The sieve of Eratosthenes**: cross out the multiples of each prime in turn on a number grid, and at 6 columns the primes line up in two columns.
- **Goldbach's comet**: count the ways to split each even number into two primes, and the counts form a comet with bands set by remainders mod 3.
- **Gaussian primes**: the primes among numbers like 3 + 2i, with their eightfold symmetry and the ordinary primes that split or stay prime.
- **The best fractions**: continued fractions as strings pulled tight around a line through the lattice, giving 22/7 and 355/113 for π and showing why the golden ratio is the hardest number to approximate.
- **Ford circles**: a circle on every fraction, touching its neighbors exactly when ad − bc = ±1, with mediants filling every gap and a zoom that never ends.
- **Pascal's triangle**: color each number by its remainder and the odd ones make a Sierpinski triangle, while each row adds up to a power of 2.
- **Factorization diagrams**: each number drawn as nested polygons of dots by its prime factors, counting up, so the primes show as plain rings.
- **A walk on π**: one step in one of ten directions for each digit of π, e or √2, against fractions whose repeating digits march off in a line.
- **Counting with dots**: proofs without words where dots rearrange to show 1 + 2 + … + n = n(n + 1)/2, odd numbers making squares and the sum of cubes.
- **Look and say**: 1, 11, 21, 1211, … read aloud, growing by Conway's constant 1.3036 and splitting into 92 kinds of pieces that never interact.

**Algebra**
- **Euler's formula**: (1 + iθ/n)^n walking around the circle to e^(iπ) = −1.
- **Matrices**: drag two arrows and watch a 2×2 matrix bend the grid, with the determinant as the area scale.
- **Eigenvectors**: sweep an arrow until the matrix only stretches it, then find the same direction by multiplying again and again.
- **Complex multiplication**: lengths multiply and angles add, with powers that spiral and the roots of 1 on a polygon.
- **Domain coloring**: complex functions painted as color wheels, with zeros and a pole you can drag.
- **Roots and coefficients**: drag roots or coefficients, and loop a coefficient around to make two roots swap places.
- **Completing the square**: x² + bx cut and rearranged into a square with one missing corner, beside the parabola it solves.
- **(a + b)³**: a square and a cube cut into pieces that pull apart into a³ + 3a²b + 3ab² + b³.
- **Perfect shuffles**: eight perfect riffle shuffles put a 52-card deck back in order, shown as card paths and loops.
- **Symmetry groups**: turn and flip a triangle or square and fill in the table of what each pair of moves makes.
- **The slide rule**: multiply by sliding two log scales, with a magnifier and the lengths that add.

**Calculus**
- **Taylor series**: polynomials copying sin x, eˣ, 1/(1 + x²) and ln(1 + x), and where they stop working.
- **Riemann sums**: rectangles and trapezoids under a curve, with the error shrinking like 1/n or 1/n² on a log chart.
- **The derivative**: a secant swinging into the tangent as h shrinks, tracing out the graph of f′ as you drag.
- **The fundamental theorem**: the area under a curve as a new function, whose slope is the curve itself.
- **Gabriel's horn**: y = 1/x spun into a 3D horn with volume π and a surface area that never stops growing.
- **The leaning tower of books**: a stack whose overhang is half the harmonic series, so it can reach as far as you like.
- **Geometric series**: halves filling a square, quarters giving 1/3, and Achilles catching the tortoise.
- **The limit game**: ε and δ as a game you always win when the limit exists, and lose at a jump.
- **Slope fields**: differential equations drawn as dashes, with solutions traced from seeds you drop.
- **Gradient descent**: a ball stepping downhill on a contour map, with learning rate, overshooting, local minima and momentum.
- **The Basel problem**: squares of area 1/n² packed into a 1 × 2 box, and the sum closing in on π²/6.

**Curves & motion**
- **Fourier circles**: spinning circles that draw any shape, including one you draw.
- **The brachistochrone**: beads racing down four tracks, where the cycloid always wins.
- **The spirograph**: gears, pen holes and the greatest common divisor.
- **The harmonograph**: drawings made by damped pendulums, and the Lissajous figures inside them.
- **The pendulum wave**: fifteen pendulums drifting through waves and lining up again each minute.
- **Bézier curves**: drag the control points and watch de Casteljau's nested in-between points draw a quadratic, cubic or quartic curve.
- **The catenary**: a simulated hanging chain that settles onto the cosh curve, not a parabola, and flips over into an arch.
- **Pursuit curves**: bugs on the corners of a polygon chase each other and spiral into the middle, each walking a distance you can work out.
- **String art**: straight threads, a sliding ladder and paper folds whose lines wrap a parabola, an astroid, an ellipse or a hyperbola.
- **The Tusi couple**: a circle rolling inside one twice its size, where every point on its rim moves in a straight line.
- **Kepler's laws**: launch a planet and see ellipses, equal areas in equal times, and T² ∝ a³ against the real planets.
- **Counting π with collisions**: two sliding blocks and a wall that clack 3, 31, 314, 3141 times as the big block gets heavier.
- **Ripples and interference**: two wave sources, or a double slit, making still lines where the waves cancel.
- **Chladni figures**: sand on a vibrating square plate gathering along the lines that stay still.
- **Standing waves on a string**: pluck a string anywhere and see which modes it plays, with the octaves and fifths they make.

**Algorithms**
- **The sorting race**: bubble, insertion, merge, quick and heap sort racing on the same bars, with comparison and swap counts and optional sound.
- **Mazes**: perfect mazes carved by the backtracker, Prim's and Wilson's methods, then solved by hand-on-the-wall or by filling dead ends.
- **Shortest paths**: breadth-first search, Dijkstra and A* finding a way across a grid of walls and mud you can draw on.
- **The travelling salesperson**: nearest neighbor, 2-opt and simulated annealing hunting for a short round trip, against the exact best for small maps.
- **The convex hull**: gift wrapping and Graham scan finding the points on the outside, and a rubber band snapping around them.
- **The Towers of Hanoi**: the recursive solution in 2ⁿ − 1 moves, a binary counter that says which disk moves next, and a version to play by hand.
- **The n queens puzzle**: a backtracking search placing queens on boards from 4 × 4 to 12 × 12, counting every solution.
- **The Hamming code**: three parity circles that find any single flipped bit, and a message sent through noise with and without the code.
- **Nim**: a game against the computer, with the binary nim-sum that tells you the winning move.
- **The prisoner's dilemma**: a round robin of strategies like tit for tat and grudger, and a population that evolves.

Plain HTML, CSS and JavaScript. No build step and no dependencies.

## Running it

Open `index.html` in a browser, or serve the folder:

```
python -m http.server 8642
```

then visit http://localhost:8642.

To publish on GitHub Pages, push the folder to a repository and enable Pages for the branch root.

## Layout

- `index.html`: page and copy for every section
- `css/style.css`: styles
- `js/core.js`: shared canvas helper (HiDPI sizing, resize, pauses animations that are off screen) and UI bindings
- `js/nav.js`: tabs, search, tags, the index and the section links, all built from the sections' `data-*` attributes
- `js/*.js`: one file per visualization

## Adding a visualization

1. Add a `<section class="viz">` to `<main>` in `index.html`. Copy an existing one and change its attributes:

   ```html
   <section class="viz" id="dragon" style="--accent: var(--pink)"
     data-group="fractals" data-tags="recursion, simple rules"
     data-short="Dragon" data-blurb="A dragon made of right angles">
   ```

   - `id`: used in links (`#dragon`) and by the script.
   - `data-group`: the tab it goes in. One of `geometry`, `topology` (Graphs & topology), `fractals`, `dynamics` (Chaos), `chance`, `numbers`, `algebra`, `calculus`, `motion` (Curves & motion) or `algorithms`.
   - `data-tags`: comma-separated. Reuse existing tags where they fit so the chips stay useful.
   - `data-short`: the label in the bar's section links.
   - `data-blurb`: the one-liner on its index card.

   The section can go anywhere in `<main>`. It's sorted into its tab automatically, after the ones already there.
2. Add `js/<id>.js` and a `<script>` tag for it at the bottom of `index.html`.
3. Add a line for it under its tab in this README.

The tabs, counts, index cards, tag chips, search and section links all update themselves. To add a new tab, add it to `GROUPS` at the top of `js/nav.js`.
