01 Four dimensions

The tesseract

A square is a line dragged sideways. A cube is a square dragged upward. Drag a cube in a fourth direction, at right angles to all three you know, and you get a tesseract: 16 corners, 32 edges and 8 cubes for faces.

We can't see four dimensions, but we can see a shadow of one. A cube's shadow can be a small square inside a big one. A tesseract's shadow is a small cube inside a big one. Rotate it through the fourth dimension and the inner cube swells until it becomes the outer one. It looks like it's turning inside out, but every cell stays a perfect cube. Only the shadow bends.

02 Rotate 90°, repeat

The dragon curve

Fold a strip of paper in half, then in half again, and again. Unfold it so every crease makes a right angle and look at it edge-on. You've made a dragon.

You don't need the paper. Start with one line. Copy the whole shape, swing the copy 90° around its last point, and attach it. Then do the same to the new shape. After a dozen rounds a coiled, clawed figure appears, and the line never once crosses itself.

Four dragons rotated around their shared starting point interlock perfectly, without a single overlap.

03 Two rules, ten thousand steps

Langton's ant

An ant walks on a grid of blank squares. On a blank square it turns 90° right, paints the square, and steps forward. On a painted square it turns 90° left, wipes it clean, and steps forward. That's the whole program.

For about 10,000 steps the result looks like noise. Then the ant starts building a "highway": a 104-step pattern that repeats and marches off diagonally forever. Every starting grid anyone has tried ends up the same way, but nobody has proved it has to.

Give the ant more colors, one turn for each, and it builds other things entirely.

04 z → z² + c

The Mandelbrot set

Pick a point c on the plane. Start at zero, square it, add c, and repeat. Either the numbers stay small forever or they run off to infinity. The points that stay small are the Mandelbrot set, painted black here. The colors show how quickly every other point escapes.

The edge never gets simpler. Zoom in anywhere along it and you find spirals, seahorses, lightning and tiny copies of the whole set, each one slightly different.

05 137.507764…°

The golden angle

Plant a seed, turn by a fixed angle, plant the next one a little farther out, and repeat. Most angles waste space: the seeds fall into straight spokes with gaps between them. One angle, about 137.5°, never lines up at all, so the seeds pack evenly. Sunflowers, daisies and pinecones grow this way.

Nudge the angle by a tenth of a degree and the spokes come back. Count the spiral arms in the golden pattern and you get Fibonacci numbers: 13, 21, 34, 55.

06 k → m·k (mod n)

Times tables on a circle

Put n points around a circle and number them. Draw a line from each point k to the point m × k, wrapping around the circle when you pass n. Times two draws a heart-shaped cardioid. Times three draws a nephroid with two lobes, times four has three.

Let m slide between whole numbers and the curves melt into one another. Some big multipliers bring back the small ones in disguise.

07 Circles riding on circles

Fourier circles

Any closed shape can be drawn by a chain of spinning circles. Each circle turns at a whole-number speed and rides on the rim of the one before it. Choose the right sizes and starting angles and the pen at the end of the chain traces your shape.

Working out those sizes is called the Fourier transform. It's one of the most useful ideas in science: it's how JPEG shrinks photos and how your phone pulls a voice out of background noise. A few circles give a rough sketch. Add more and the corners sharpen.

08 Sensitive dependence

The butterfly effect

In 1963 Edward Lorenz boiled the weather down to three equations and let a computer follow them. The point never settles and never repeats itself. It loops around two wings forever.

Start two paths a hundred-thousandth apart. For a while they move as one. Then the gap explodes and they go completely separate ways. That's why forecasts more than a couple of weeks out are guesswork: tiny errors in today's measurements grow until they swamp everything.

09 Patterns in the primes

Prime spirals

Place every whole number n at distance n from the center, turned n radians. Keep only the primes. Spirals appear. Zoom out and there are dozens of them. Zoom out further and they straighten into rays.

The arms come from fractions that are very close to 2π: 44/7 and 710/113. Numbers 44 apart, or 710 apart, point in almost the same direction, so they line up. Primes skip every arm whose numbers share a factor with 44 or 710, which is why some arms are empty. Even the first 6 arms show it: every prime above 3 sits next to a multiple of 6, so primes only use 2 of them.

10 Random rolls, perfect order

The chaos game

Mark the three corners of a triangle and put a dot anywhere. Roll a die to pick a corner, move halfway toward it, and mark the spot. Then do it again from there. Every move is random, yet after a few thousand rolls the dots form the Sierpinski triangle: a triangle full of triangle-shaped holes, all the way down.

Change the shape or the rule and other fractals appear. Swap the corners for four stretch-and-shift rules and the same random process grows a fern.

11 Where randomness piles up

The bell curve

Drop a ball onto rows of pegs. At every peg it has an even chance of bouncing left or right, so where any one ball lands is pure luck. Drop thousands and the pile always takes the same shape: the bell curve, also called the normal distribution.

It appears because most paths mix lefts and rights about evenly, and only a few go all one way. Heights, test scores and measurement errors pile up the same way for the same reason: each is the sum of many small random nudges.
